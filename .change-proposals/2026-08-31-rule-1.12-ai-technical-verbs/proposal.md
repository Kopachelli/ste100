## In one line

Issue 9 added artificial intelligence **nouns** to rule 1.5 and no matching
**verbs** to rule 1.12, so a writer documenting an AI system must break either
rule 1.3 or rule 3.7 to describe any action in it.

## Writing rule or dictionary word

Rule 1.12, technical verbs. Category 2, Computer processes and applications.

## Change that you think is necessary

Add a new subcategory 2 d) to rule 1.12. Use the same format as the
subcategories 2 a) thru 2 c).

> **2. Computer processes and applications**
> Terms that give instructions and information for:
> **d) Artificial intelligence and machine learning processes:**
> annotate, embed, fine-tune, infer, label, prompt, train

Examples in STE:

> Annotate the images before you train the model.
> Train the model with the applicable data.
> Prompt the model with the instructions for the task.
> Fine-tune the model for your subject field.
> The system embeds the text and keeps the result in the database.

## Purpose of the change

Issue 9 added artificial intelligence terms to rule 1.5, category 19. Examples
are "artificial intelligence", "deep learning", "embedding", "hallucination",
"large language model", "machine learning", "prompt engineering", "token", and
"tuning".

Rule 1.12, category 2, does not include the related technical verbs. Its three
subcategories give verbs for input and output processes, for user interface
processes, and for system operations. No subcategory gives verbs for artificial
intelligence processes.

Thus a writer who documents an AI-enabled system has only two options. Each
option disagrees with a rule.

The first option is to use an approved word with a meaning that the dictionary
does not approve. TUNE (v) has the approved meaning "to adjust equipment to the
best performance". DEPLOY (v) has an approved meaning for movement out of a
position of storage and into operation. A model is not equipment, and a model is
not in storage. Thus "tune the model" and "deploy the model" disagree with rule
1.3. The risk is high, because rule 1.5 already gives the related nouns "tuning"
and "artificial intelligence".

The second option is a noun construction for each action, for example "do the
tuning of the model". Rule 1.12 correctly tells the writer to use an approved
verb when the dictionary has one. But the dictionary has no approved verb for
these actions. And rule 3.7 tells the writer to use a verb for an action, not a
noun.

The proposed subcategory removes this condition. It is an addition to a
structure that the standard already contains, and rule 1.12 tells the reader
that the categories give examples only. Category 2 c) gives the precedent. It
includes the verbs "boot", "debug", and "reboot", which the dictionary does not
approve.

The change also agrees with the STEMG white paper on artificial intelligence of
June 2026. That white paper says that STE must stay the primary reference in
AI-related technical communication. A writer can only do this if STE gives the
necessary words.

## Why this proposal and not another

The brief was one change with a real chance of acceptance. The reasoning:

**It fixes an inconsistency the standard created, rather than asking the
standard to change its mind.** Issue 9 introduced the gap. A proposal that
completes work already begun is easier to accept than one that reverses a
decision.

**It is additive and reversible.** Nothing existing changes meaning. No approved
word is redefined. No rule is rewritten. The dictionary is untouched.

**It uses the standard's own escape hatch.** Rule 1.12 states that its
categories give examples only, and rule 1.5 says the same for the nouns. Adding
examples to an open list is the change the structure anticipates.

**It has an internal precedent.** Category 2 c) already lists `boot`, `debug`
and `reboot` as technical verbs that the dictionary does not approve. The
request is the same move, one subject field over.

**It lands where the group is already working.** The STEMG formed an AI Task
Team and published a white paper in June 2026. This is a concrete, small item on
that team's own subject.

Changes that were considered and rejected: relaxing the semicolon ban (rule 8.1
is foundational); permitting the present perfect (rule 3.2 is foundational);
widening the approved meaning of TUNE (v) or DEPLOY (v) (a dictionary change
with wider consequences than a rule example); and adding non-aerospace examples
to the dictionary (invited on page 2-0-14 and near-certain to be accepted, but
with almost no substance).
